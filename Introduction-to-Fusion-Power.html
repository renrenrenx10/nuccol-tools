A Different Technology, A Different Supply Chain

Introduction to Fusion Power

Fusion isn't just "nuclear but newer" — it's a fundamentally different process with its own technologies, challenges and emerging supply chain opportunities for manufacturers.

6 min readTraining & Development

01Fusion fundamentals

Nuclear fusion is the process of combining atomic nuclei to release energy — the opposite process to fission, which splits nuclei apart. Fusion's potential advantages are what make it attractive as a future energy source: an abundant fuel source (deuterium and tritium, extractable from seawater), no greenhouse gas emissions, inherent safety features, and minimal long-lived radioactive waste compared to fission.

02The main approaches to fusion

ApproachMethodExamples
Magnetic confinement fusionUses powerful magnetic fields to contain superheated plasmaTokamaks, stellarators
Inertial confinement fusionUses lasers or ion beams to rapidly compress and heat a small fuel targetLaser-driven and ion-beam-driven systems

03Current status and key challenges

Major fusion experiments including ITER, JET and NIF have made significant progress, but key challenges remain before fusion becomes commercially viable: achieving sustained fusion reactions, developing materials that can withstand the extreme conditions inside a fusion reactor, and scaling up the technology for commercial power generation.

04Potential applications beyond electricity

  • Electricity generation — the primary long-term application.
  • Hydrogen production — fusion heat and electricity could support large-scale green hydrogen production.
  • Desalination — high-grade heat output has potential desalination applications.
  • Space exploration — fusion propulsion concepts are an active area of research interest.

05Supply chain opportunities for SMEs

The fusion industry is still emerging, which means the supply chain is less established and more open to new entrants than mature fission markets.

  • Materials — fusion reactors require materials able to withstand extreme heat and neutron flux, an active area of specialist materials development.
  • Manufacturing — precision manufacturing of magnets, vacuum vessels and plasma-facing components.
  • Diagnostics — sensors and measurement systems capable of operating in extreme fusion plasma environments.
  • Key players span both research institutions (ITER, national labs) and a growing number of private fusion companies — SMEs have a genuine role in accelerating development, not just serving an established market.

06A realistic first step

Understand where your existing capability might transferExtreme-environment materials, precision magnet or vacuum-vessel manufacturing, and specialist sensor/diagnostics work are the areas most likely to have transferable relevance.
Watch private fusion companies as well as public research programmesA growing number of privately funded fusion ventures are building supply chains now, earlier and potentially faster-moving than public programme procurement.
Treat this as an early-stage, higher-risk opportunityFusion is not yet a mature commercial market — engage with realistic expectations about timelines, alongside the genuine first-mover advantage of engaging early.

07Frequently asked questions

Is fusion the same as fission for supply chain purposes?

No — fusion uses different fuels, different reactor concepts and faces different engineering challenges (extreme heat and plasma confinement rather than fission's core challenges), so fission supply chain experience doesn't automatically transfer.

How close is fusion to commercial deployment?

Estimates vary and have historically proven optimistic — the honest answer is that sustained, commercially viable fusion power generation remains a research and engineering challenge, not a near-term certainty.
