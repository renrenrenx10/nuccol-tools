Practical Guide for SMEs Without a Marketing Team

Marketing for Manufacturers

You don't need a marketing department to win nuclear work. You need a clear message, a few consistent channels, and proof that you can deliver — here's how to build all three without a big budget.

9 min readGrowth & Marketing

01Start with your value proposition

Before any channel, campaign or website redesign, be able to answer one question in a single sentence: why should a nuclear buyer choose you over the next supplier on the list? That might be a certification, a niche capability, your location, your track record, your lead times, or simply your capacity to scale with them as they grow.

Most manufacturers find this harder than expected precisely because they know their own business so well — everything feels important. The trick is to pick the one or two things that a buyer, reading in thirty seconds, would remember afterwards.

A useful testAsk a colleague who doesn't work in sales to read your website homepage for ten seconds, then look away and tell you what you do and why they'd trust you. If they can't, your value proposition isn't landing yet.

03What your website actually needs to do

  • State clearly, above the fold, what you make and who for — not a generic 'engineering excellence' strapline.
  • Show proof: certifications (F4N, ISO), notable customers (even described generically if confidentiality requires it), and years of operation.
  • Make it obvious how to get in touch — a named contact and a real phone number beats a contact form buried three clicks deep.
  • Keep it current — an outdated 'News' section from three years ago undermines trust more than having no news section at all.

04Proof beats polish

Nuclear buyers are risk-averse by design — that's a feature of the sector, not a flaw in your pitch. A short, specific case study — what you did, for whom (even anonymised if needed), and the outcome — will do more work than an expensive brochure. If you hold an F4N certificate, a quality accreditation, or a notable customer relationship, put it above the fold on your homepage, not buried in an 'About Us' page.

A simple case study format that worksOne paragraph: the challenge the customer had, what you delivered, and one measurable outcome (on time, to spec, reduced lead time, etc.). You don't need permission to describe the type of work if you keep the customer's name out of it.

05A realistic quarterly marketing rhythm

WhenActivity
Month 1Refresh website proof points; publish one LinkedIn post built around a recent piece of work
Month 2Attend, or follow up from, one Meet the Buyer style event or sector Summit
Month 3Send one email update to your contact list; review what actually generated a response

06Measuring what matters, not what's easy to count

  • Enquiries that mention a specific capability of yours (a sign your message is landing with the right people)
  • Repeat engagement from the same buyer contact over time, even before a contract materialises
  • Website visits arriving from procurement-relevant searches, rather than raw traffic volume alone

07Common mistakes SMEs make here

✗Trying to be everywhere at once instead of doing three channels well.
✗Writing marketing copy about the company rather than about the customer's problem.
✗Letting the website go stale after the initial launch, so it quietly undermines the message it once made.
✗Measuring only website traffic, and missing the quieter signals — repeat contact, specific enquiries — that actually predict a sale.

08Frequently asked questions

I have no marketing budget at all — where do I start?

Start with the website's homepage and one LinkedIn post about recent work. Both cost time, not money, and both directly support the 'proof beats polish' principle above.

Is LinkedIn really worth it for a manufacturing SME?

Yes, specifically because procurement and engineering contacts in this sector use it actively to research suppliers before ever picking up the phone.

How do I get case studies if my customers won't let me name them?

Describe the type of work, sector and outcome without naming the customer — 'a Tier 1 supplier to an SMR developer' still carries credibility.
