What this model is, in plain English
The short version is the headings. Open any section for the detail behind it.

The short version

This model answers one question: if someone builds a power station, what physical things do they need to buy, and when? Concrete, steel, valves, pumps, cable, and the specialist items that only exist in nuclear and hydrogen plants.

It currently covers — different materials and components across — reactor designs, plus fusion and hydrogen plant. Every single number traces back to a named published document — there are — sources behind it, listed on the References screen.

What it is not: a quote, a shopping list, or a procurement specification. It is an informed estimate of the scale and timing of demand, good enough to plan supply chain capacity against. It is not good enough to order from.

Where the numbers come from

Four kinds of source, strongest first.

Design documents filed with nuclear regulators

When a company wants to build a reactor, it has to file an enormous technical submission with the regulator — the NRC in America, the ONR here. Those are public. They state how many steam generators a plant has, how many fuel assemblies, how much concrete the shield building takes. Where we have a figure from one of these, it is as good as it gets without being the vendor.

Plants that have actually been built

Design documents say what is intended; completed projects say what it really took. We use published outturn figures from Flamanville 3 in France — the only finished Western EPR — and from the Hinkley Point C and Sizewell C planning applications. This is unusual: most published models do not have real construction figures at this level of detail.

Government and agency statistics

For the question "could the UK actually supply this?", we use official statistics — UK trade data, the British Geological Survey, the US Geological Survey for world mining output, and the International Atomic Energy Agency for plant benchmarks.

Manufacturers' own published specifications

For the hydrogen equipment, the figures come from GeoPura's and Ballard's own published technical specifications and from Ballard's investor presentation. Nothing is taken from conversations — we have not spoken to them. Everything here could be checked by anyone with a web browser.

What each build in the model is, and what it is missing

Sizewell C — the strongest one

A 1,630 MW EPR. This is the most reliable entry in the model, because our reference plant was built on EPR data in the first place — so almost no adjustment is needed to represent Sizewell C. If anyone asks which number to trust most, it is this one.

Missing: nothing structural. The usual caveat applies — these are estimates, not a bill of materials.

Cottam — a small modular reactor

A Holtec SMR-300, 300 MW. Reasonable confidence. It is scaled down from our small-reactor reference plant, which was built on Rolls-Royce SMR data — a different design, but the same family of technology at a similar size.

Missing: Holtec have published far less than the American vendors have, so there is less design-specific detail behind it than behind Sizewell C.

STEP at West Burton — fusion, and therefore the most uncertain

STEP does not exist yet. Nobody has built a fusion power station. The figures here are worked out from UKAEA's own published design notes and from ITER, the international fusion project in France, scaled to STEP's published machine size.

One thing worth knowing: fusion plant does not scale by electrical output the way a fission plant does. STEP is physically larger than ITER but produces a fraction of the power. Sizing it on megawatts would have understated it by roughly five times, so it is sized on the machine itself.

Missing: several lines are deliberately marked as floors rather than totals — we can prove at least this much is needed, and the real number is higher. Valves and tanks especially.

GeoPura hydrogen power units — thin, and honestly so

A GeoPura HPU is a 250 kW generator in a shipping container that runs on hydrogen instead of diesel. We model four things in it: the battery, the fuel cell stacks, the platinum in those stacks, and the steel structure.

Missing, and this is the important bit: the whole unit weighs 17.2 tonnes and we have only broken down about 3 tonnes of it. Roughly 14 tonnes per unit — the inverter, the cooling, the pipework — is not itemised, because that needs a parts list only GeoPura or Siemens Energy has.

A finding worth repeating: the fuel cell is under 1% of the unit's weight. Two stacks is about 130 kg out of 17,200. The fuel cell is the headline, but the tonnage is in everything around it.

HyMarnham — hydrogen production

GeoPura's hydrogen plant in Nottinghamshire. 15 MW of electrolysers, producing about 6 tonnes of hydrogen a day, half-owned by GeoPura. We know from Ballard's investor presentation that these are alkaline electrolysers, which matters: the alternative technology would need titanium, iridium and platinum instead of nickel. Different technology, completely different shopping list.

Note: GeoPura's own website still says 8 MW. The 15 MW figure is newer and comes from the investor presentation.

The two reference plants

Underneath everything sit two synthetic plants — one large, one small. They were assembled by merging the design documents of eighteen real reactors, removing duplicates, and keeping the best-sourced figure for each item. Every named design in the model is then sized from the appropriate reference plant.

The limitation this creates: two plants of the same electrical output will produce very similar numbers, even if one is a boiling water reactor and the other a pressurised water reactor. Where a difference is genuinely known — steam generator tubing, for instance, which boiling water reactors do not have at all — it is handled explicitly. Elsewhere, size is doing the work.

Things that will look wrong but are not

The chart only shows one item at a time

You cannot add concrete to valves — one is cubic metres, the other is a count. So the chart draws one item across all the builds, and you pick which. The list on the right is how you change it.

A build sometimes vanishes from the chart

Builds only appear for items they actually use. Put concrete on the chart and the hydrogen units disappear, because a container-sized generator does not pour concrete. Switch to platinum and the reactors disappear instead. Nothing is broken — the build simply has no demand for that item.

Some numbers are floors, not totals

Where we can prove a minimum but not a total, the model says so rather than guessing. Fusion valves are an example: we can count 24 large ones in the published design and we know there are more. Treat those as "at least this many".

Every number has a range, not just a single figure

Each item carries a low, a likely and a high. The single number on screen is the likely one. Where sources disagree, the range widens — which is itself useful information about how firm the figure is.

Why UK capacity is sometimes blank

For some items there is simply no UK producer and no meaningful statistic — fusion magnet components, for instance. Rather than invent a figure, the model says what it looked at and found nothing. That gap is a finding in itself.

If you remember one thing

Every figure in here is traceable to a published document, and where we do not know something, the model says so rather than filling the gap. That is the point of it. The gaps are not embarrassments — they are precisely the questions worth putting to a developer, and they are the reason to have the conversation in the first place.

Full source list on the References screen. Every individual figure shows its own source on the Products screen.

How to use it
Five steps to your first chart. Everything else is optional.

Five steps to your first chart

  1. Go to the Search tab — the first one along the top.
  2. Choose something from the Developer list. Try EDF — Sizewell C.
  3. Set a Start Date, then say how many you are building. Concurrent means side by side at the same time; Consecutive means one after another. Sizewell C is two reactors built together, so that is Concurrent 2, Consecutive 1.
  4. Click ＋ Add to Plan. You can repeat steps 2 to 4 to add more builds and compare them.
  5. Click ▶ Generate Demand, then 📊 View Chart. The chart opens in a new tab.

That is the whole thing. The rest of this page is only worth reading if something puzzles you.

If something puzzles you

Concurrent and consecutive — what is the difference?

Concurrent is how many are built at the same time. Consecutive is how many are built one after the other, each starting as the one before it finishes.

Two reactors going up together at Sizewell C: Concurrent 2, Consecutive 1 — one big peak of demand.

A factory turning out ten units a year for six years: Concurrent 10, Consecutive 6 — a long flat run instead of a peak.

It matters because the total is the same either way, but when the demand lands is completely different, and that is the thing a supplier needs to know.

The chart shows one item at a time — how do I change it?

The list down the right-hand side is every material and component. Click any of them and the chart redraws for that one. Above that list is a dropdown marked Filter list, set to All to begin with. Change it to narrow the list to one group — just the concrete and civils, say, or just the electrical items — which makes finding something a lot quicker.

It only ever shows one item because you cannot add cubic metres of concrete to a count of valves — they are different things measured in different units.

One of my builds has vanished from the chart

It has not gone wrong. Builds only appear for items they actually use. Put concrete on the chart and the GeoPura hydrogen units disappear, because a generator in a shipping container does not pour concrete. Switch to platinum and the reactors disappear instead.

If you want to see everything a build uses, the ▦ Data table button at the top of the chart lists every item at once.

What the chart buttons do

⧉ Overlay draws every build from zero, so you can compare them against each other. ≡ Stacked piles them on top of each other, so you see the combined total. Overlay also draws a thicker line for the total across all builds; stacked does not, because the top of the pile already is the total.

▐█ Bar and ◿ Area are just two ways of drawing the same numbers — pick whichever reads better.

Quarter and Year change how finely time is sliced. Year is usually easier to look at.

🏭 Nuclear capacity, ⚙ UK output and ⏱ Lead Time lay extra information over the top — roughly, can Britain make this, and how long does it take to get.

↩ Reset puts everything back if you get lost.

Getting the numbers out into Excel

On the Search tab, once you have generated demand, there are two buttons. ⬇ Raw CSV gives you every single row — developer, item, year, quarter, amount, unit. ⬇ Pivot CSV gives you a tidier summary by category. Both open straight in Excel.

What the other tabs are for

Products — every material and component in the model, with the quantity for a large plant and a small one, and the source each figure came from.

Developers — the builds available to choose from, and what each is based on.

Schedules — when during a build each item is needed. Concrete early, control systems late.

UK Capacity — for each item, whether Britain can supply it and roughly how much per year. This is where the supply chain gaps show up.

References — every source behind the model, and how reliable each one is.

Plain English — what the model is and what it cannot tell you.

Starting again

🗑 Clear All on the Search tab empties your plan so you can start a fresh one. Nothing is saved anywhere and nothing can be broken — if it ever looks wrong, close the browser tab and open index.html again.

Nothing here sends anything anywhere. It all runs on your own machine, and closing it loses nothing you cannot make again in thirty seconds.
