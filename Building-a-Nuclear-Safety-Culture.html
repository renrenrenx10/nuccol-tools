Building a Nuclear Safety Culture

The INPO/WANO 'Ten Traits' framework, explained in depth for an SME context — what each trait actually looks like in a small manufacturing business.

8 min readSafety Culture

01What 'safety culture' actually means

Safety culture isn't a poster on the wall or a line in a policy document — it's the sum of the everyday, often unspoken assumptions and behaviours that shape how people in your business actually act when nobody's watching. The INPO/WANO Ten Traits framework, widely used across the international nuclear industry, breaks this into ten observable traits across three categories, giving you something concrete to assess and build, rather than an abstract aspiration.

02Individual Commitment to Safety

Personal accountabilityEveryone knows their own role in keeping things safe — not just 'someone else's job'. In an SME, this often means an operator feeling genuinely responsible for flagging a concern, not just following instructions.
Questioning attitudePeople are encouraged to ask 'is this right?' rather than assume it is, even when under time pressure. A healthy sign is a junior team member feeling comfortable pausing a job to check something.
Effective safety communicationConcerns and lessons are shared openly, not filtered upward or softened before reaching management.

03Management Commitment to Safety

Leadership safety values and actionsLeaders visibly prioritise safety, especially when it's inconvenient — for example, accepting a delay to fix an issue rather than pushing through a deadline.
Decision-makingSafety is weighed explicitly in decisions, not assumed to be 'fine' by default. This shows up as a documented rationale, not just a gut call.
Respectful work environmentPeople feel able to raise issues without fear of blame — the test is whether someone would raise a mistake themselves before being asked.

04Management Systems

Continuous learningNear-misses and mistakes are treated as learning opportunities, with lessons actually fed back into practice, not just recorded.
Problem identification and resolutionIssues are tracked to genuine closure, not just logged and forgotten once the immediate pressure passes.
Environment for raising concernsThere's a clear, safe route to raise a concern at any level, and people know what it is without having to ask.
Work processesProcedures reflect how work is actually done, and are kept current — a sign of trouble is when 'the way we really do it' differs from what's written down.

05A simple way to start assessing your own culture

Pick three of the ten traits above that feel most relevant to your business right now, and ask a handful of people at different levels — not just managers — how they'd honestly rate the business against each one. The gaps between how different levels of the business see things are often more revealing than any single score.

06Frequently asked questions

Do we need a formal safety culture programme to get started?

No — start with honest conversations against the ten traits above. Formal programmes can follow once you understand where your real gaps are.
