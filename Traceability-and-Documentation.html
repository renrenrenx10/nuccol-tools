Why It's Non-Negotiable in Nuclear

Traceability & Documentation

In most industries, missing paperwork is an inconvenience. In nuclear, it can mean a licensee can no longer prove a component is safe &mdash; here's what regulators actually check, and how to build traceability into daily practice.

8 min readSupply Chain & Quality

01Why nuclear treats documentation as a safety issue

The Office for Nuclear Regulation (ONR) regulates UK nuclear licensees against a set of Licence Conditions. Licence Condition 6 (Documents, records, authorities and certificates) requires that the licensee "make adequate records to demonstrate compliance with any of the conditions attached to this licence", and that those records, once approved, are preserved for 30 years unless ONR agrees otherwise.

That obligation doesn't stop at the licensee's own gate — it flows down through the supply chain. If your component fails ten years after installation, someone needs to be able to trace exactly what it was, who made it, what it was tested to, and who signed it off. If that record doesn't exist, or can't be found, the licensee has a genuine safety and regulatory problem — and it traces straight back to you.

02What LC6 actually requires, in plain terms

RequirementWhat it means for you as a supplier
Adequate records of complianceYou need to be able to show, with paperwork, that what you delivered met the specification — not just assert that it did
30-year preservationRecords can't be treated as disposable once a job is invoiced and closed — the retention period is decades, not years
ONR approval of arrangementsFor the highest safety-significance work, your customer's own record-keeping arrangements (which your evidence feeds into) may themselves be subject to ONR approval
No unapproved changes to approved arrangementsIf a customer's documented process for handling your records is ONR-approved, they can't quietly change it without going back to the regulator
ONR inspectors have carried out real, published compliance inspections against LC6 (and its close companion, LC25 — Operational records) at major sites including Sellafield — this isn't a theoretical requirement, it's actively checked.

03Traceability and CFSI — two sides of the same coin

Traceability isn't just about proving your own good work — it's the primary defence against Counterfeit, Fraudulent and Suspect Items (CFSI) entering the nuclear supply chain. The Nuclear Industry Safety Directors Forum defines CFSI as goods illegally labelled and represented as original, and warns that almost anything can be copied — including machined parts.

Why this matters more in nuclear than elsewhereA counterfeit part can look identical to the genuine article from the outside, while containing different materials or skipping the testing the real part underwent. Full traceability — knowing the genuine provenance of every part, material and record — is the main practical defence, because it makes a counterfeit substitution visible before it's installed, not after something fails.

For engineering and design roles, that means defining technical specifications, critical characteristics and acceptance criteria as early as possible. For procurement, it means passing those requirements down through your own supply chain so they form part of goods acceptance — and, wherever practical, trading directly with the manufacturer rather than through unverified intermediaries.

04What good traceability looks like in practice

  • A unique, unbroken reference linking raw material, in-process records, test/inspection results and the finished item — so any one of them leads you back to the others.
  • Records created at the point of work, not reconstructed afterwards from memory or a rough note.
  • A clear owner for record retention — someone actually responsible for making sure records exist for the full retention period, not just until the invoice is paid.
  • Certificates and authorities kept alongside the physical or digital record they relate to, not in a separate system nobody remembers to check.
  • A documented process for what happens when a record is found to be missing or wrong — not just prevention, but a defined response.

05A realistic path to building this in

Stage 1 — Map your current record trailWalk one recent job from raw material through to delivery and see whether you could actually reconstruct full traceability today. Most gaps are found here, not invented.
Stage 2 — Fix the weakest link firstTraceability is only as strong as its weakest connection — usually the handover between two systems or two people, not any single record itself.
Stage 3 — Build a retention plan, not just a filing habitDecide, in writing, who owns record retention and for how long, rather than relying on records surviving by accident.
Stage 4 — Treat CFSI awareness as part of the same systemTraceability and counterfeit-item awareness reinforce each other — train the same people on both together rather than as separate topics.
Stage 5 — Ask your customer what they actually need to seeRequirements vary by grade and criticality (see the Nuclear Standards Quick Guide for ISO 19443 grading) — confirm what's expected rather than assuming the highest bar always applies.

06Common mistakes SMEs make here

✗Treating traceability as paperwork to complete just before an audit, rather than a routine part of daily work.
✗Losing traceability at a handover point — between departments, between systems, or between your business and a sub-supplier.
✗Assuming retention obligations end when the contract does — LC6's 30-year preservation requirement runs long past project closeout.
✗Not knowing where records for a specific job actually are, even when they technically exist somewhere.

07Frequently asked questions

Does LC6 apply directly to my business, or only to the licensee?

LC6 is a legal condition on the licensee (e.g. the site operator), but it can only be satisfied if the records your work depends on actually exist and are adequate — so the practical obligation flows down to you as a supplier.

How long do I really need to keep records for?

The standard reference point is 30 years unless ONR has approved a different period for the specific arrangement — treat this as the default assumption unless your customer tells you otherwise.

What's the difference between LC6 and LC25?

LC6 covers documents, records, authorities and certificates broadly. LC25 (Operational records) specifically covers records of the operation, inspection and maintenance of plant that may affect safety — the two are usually inspected together.

How is this different from the Spotting Counterfeit Parts guide?

They're closely related — traceability is the main practical defence against CFSI. This guide focuses on the documentation and record-keeping obligation itself; the CFSI guide focuses on recognising and responding to suspect items.
