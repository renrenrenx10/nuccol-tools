Know What You Can Actually Deliver

Capacity and Capability Planning

Winning nuclear work is only half the challenge — proving you can deliver it at the volume and quality required is what buyers actually assess before they award. Here's how to plan for it.

7 min readTraining & Development

01Why buyers scrutinise capacity separately from capability

Capability answers "can you make this?" Capacity answers "can you make enough of this, reliably, alongside everything else you already do?" Nuclear buyers assess both separately because a supplier with the right skills but insufficient spare capacity is just as much a delivery risk as one lacking the skills entirely.

02Capacity planning fundamentals

  • Understand your current utilisation — know what proportion of your capacity is already committed before quoting for new work.
  • Model demand realistically — nuclear programmes often have long lead times followed by concentrated delivery windows; plan for peaks, not just averages.
  • Build in contingency — equipment downtime, staff absence and rework all eat into theoretical capacity; buyers expect to see this accounted for, not assumed away.

03Assessing your own capacity honestly

Map your production/delivery capacity by process stepIdentify the actual bottleneck in your process, not just headline output — most operations have one constraining step that governs true throughput.
Track capacity utilisation over time, not just headline outputA single good month doesn't prove sustained capacity — buyers on multi-year frameworks want evidence of consistency.
Be explicit about what would need to change to scale upAdditional shifts, equipment, subcontracting or premises — know your own scale-up plan before a buyer asks about it.

04Assessing supplier and partner capacity

If you rely on subcontractors or key suppliers to deliver nuclear work, their capacity constraints become your capacity constraints from the buyer's perspective. A capacity assessment that stops at your own gates understates the real risk in your supply chain.

  • Ask key suppliers directly about their own current utilisation and capacity headroom.
  • Understand where a single point of failure exists — one sole-source supplier with no spare capacity is a live risk to flag and manage.
  • Build these assessments into your own risk register, not just a one-off supplier onboarding exercise.

05Resource allocation and optimisation

  • Prioritise resource allocation against contractual commitments and penalties, not just revenue value.
  • Use scheduling and planning tools proportionate to your scale — a spreadsheet used consistently beats an expensive system used badly.
  • Review allocation regularly as new work is won, not just at the point of quoting.

06Continuous improvement

Capacity and capability planning isn't a one-off exercise done at tender stage — the strongest suppliers treat it as an ongoing discipline, reviewing actual versus planned utilisation after each contract and feeding lessons back into how the next capacity assessment is done.

07Common mistakes SMEs make here

✗Quoting for new work based on theoretical maximum capacity rather than realistic, contingency-adjusted capacity.
✗Not accounting for a key subcontractor's own capacity constraints when committing to a delivery schedule.
✗Treating capacity assessment as a one-off tender exercise rather than an ongoing operational discipline.
✗Overpromising scale-up speed without a concrete, costed plan for how that scale-up would actually happen.
