What SCRL and demand modelling actually mean for you

Demystifying Supply Chain Readiness

SCRL &mdash; Supply Chain Readiness Level &mdash; is NucCol's own methodology for understanding how ready UK manufacturers are for nuclear work, at scale.

6 min readSupply Chain

01What SCRL actually is

SCRL stands for Supply Chain Readiness Level. It's a NucCol methodology, inspired by the idea of Technology Readiness Levels (TRLs), that gives a structured, RAG-rated (Red/Amber/Green) picture of how ready a company or a sector is to take on nuclear supply chain work.

02What it assesses

  • Quality management maturity
  • Operational capability and capacity
  • Financial stability
  • Health & safety performance
  • Workforce capacity and skills pipeline
  • Sector-specific compliance (e.g. nuclear-specific standards)

03How it's scored, and where the data comes from

Each area is given a RAG rating, built from F4N assessment data, NucCol's supplier portal of 3,500+ companies, and targeted engagement with individual businesses. The result is a version-controlled workbook and report — not a one-off snapshot, but something that can be tracked and updated over time.

04Who uses SCRL, and why

UserHow they use it
ManufacturersUnderstand their own gaps and track improvement
Primes & Tier 1sAssess the depth and resilience of their supply base
Government & industry bodiesUnderstand sector-wide capacity and where investment is needed

05How this relates to item-level quality grading

SCRL looks at a company's overall readiness. That's a different, narrower question from how a specific part or component is graded for procurement. That's where the NSC1/NSC2/NSC3/NN framework comes in:

GradeWhat it means
NSC1Highest nuclear safety class — most stringent controls
NSC2High nuclear safety relevance
NSC3Some nuclear safety relevance
NN (Non-Nuclear)Standard commercial procurement controls apply

06What demand modelling actually means

Demand modelling is the exercise of forecasting how much of a given capability the sector will need — by commodity type — over the coming years, so that gaps can be identified and investment (in skills, capacity, certification) can be targeted before a shortage becomes a bottleneck.
