Tools That Catch Human Error Before It Matters

Human Performance in Nuclear Operations

Most failures don't start with bad people — they start with good people under pressure making human mistakes. Here's how nuclear organisations build in tools to catch that before it becomes an incident.

8 min readTraining & Development

01Why human performance is managed as a system

In nuclear, small errors can have large consequences. A mature organisation treats human performance tools and error prevention as standard daily practice, not an occasional add-on for high-risk tasks only.

02Core human performance tools

STAR (Stop, Think, Act, Review)

A simple discipline for pausing before a task step, thinking through the intended action and its risks, acting deliberately, then reviewing the outcome before moving on.

Three-way communication

The receiver repeats back an instruction in their own words and the sender confirms it's correct — closing the loop on verbal instructions that are easy to mishear.

Pre-job briefs and post-job reviews

Structured discussion before a task starts (what could go wrong, who's doing what) and after it finishes (what actually happened, what to change next time).

Peer checks / independent verification

A second, independent person checks safety-critical work before it's signed off, rather than relying on a single person's self-check.

Time-outs / stop-work authority

Any team member can pause work if something doesn't look or feel right, without needing management approval first.

03Tailoring tools to role and environment

  • Adapt tools to fit shop floor, engineering and admin roles differently — a tool that works for a repetitive production task may need adapting for a one-off critical engineering task.
  • Recognise high-pressure versus steady-state work needs different levels of formality — build that flexibility in rather than a single rigid process.
  • Make tools practical with real examples relevant to your own operation, not abstract theory.

04Measuring and reinforcing usage

A tool that exists in a procedure but isn't actually used doesn't reduce risk. Supervisors should observe and give feedback on use of pre-job briefs, communication discipline and situational awareness, and human performance tool usage should be part of routine audit and assurance checks — with incident learning feeding back into which tools were or weren't used and why.

05Fatigue management and workload control

Performance, decision-making and safety all collapse when people are running on empty — fatigue management is a planning discipline, not an afterthought.

Design work patterns with fatigue in mindClear limits on working hours, rest breaks, shift lengths and rotation cycles, following HSE and sector-specific guidance.
Monitor actual workload in real timeTrack hours worked and overtime, including travel and on-call duties, and give supervisors authority to stop work or rotate people out when needed.
Train staff to recognise and report fatigueCover the signs of fatigue and how to report concerns without stigma — including fitness-for-duty checks as part of pre-job briefs.
Build fatigue into planningRisk assessments, staffing plans for complex work, and contingency planning for late shifts or delays.

06Monitoring human performance to drive improvement

  • Define what good human performance looks like — procedural adherence, communication discipline, use of the tools above, safety-first decision-making.
  • Observe performance proactively through supervisor coaching, peer review of high-risk tasks and human performance walkdowns.
  • Analyse near-misses and behavioural trends (rule-bending, last-minute workarounds) to focus improvement effort.
  • Ask teams directly where they're most likely to make mistakes, rather than assuming leadership already knows.

07Common mistakes SMEs make here

✗Treating human performance tools as an occasional add-on for high-risk tasks rather than standard daily practice.
✗Running root cause analysis after an incident but not checking which human performance tools were or weren't used.
✗Monitoring workload on paper (contracted hours) without tracking real overtime, travel and on-call time that contributes to fatigue.
✗Punishing rather than learning from self-reported near-misses, which discourages honest reporting.
